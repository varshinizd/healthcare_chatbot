METFORMIN
Metformin is the first-line oral antidiabetic medication for type 2 diabetes. It belongs to the biguanide class and works primarily by reducing hepatic glucose production and improving insulin sensitivity. It does not cause hypoglycemia when used as monotherapy. Common side effects include gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, diarrhea, abdominal discomfort), which are minimized by taking with food and using extended-release formulations. Contraindications include significant renal impairment (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m²), severe hepatic impairment, and conditions predisposing to lactic acidosis. It should be temporarily discontinued before procedures using iodinated contrast. Dosage must be individualized and confirmed with a healthcare provider.

STATINS (HMG-COA REDUCTASE INHIBITORS)
Statins are the cornerstone of lipid-lowering therapy and cardiovascular risk reduction. They work by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase, reducing LDL cholesterol. Common agents include atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, simvastatin, and pravastatin. High-intensity statins (atorvastatin 40-80mg, rosuvastatin 20-40mg) reduce LDL by ≥50%. Side effects include myalgia (muscle pain), elevated liver enzymes, and rarely rhabdomyolysis. Statins are contraindicated in pregnancy and active liver disease. Drug interactions with CYP3A4 inhibitors (grapefruit juice, clarithromycin) can increase statin levels. Specific dosing requires physician determination based on cardiovascular risk profile.

ANTIBIOTICS - GENERAL PRINCIPLES
Antibiotics are medications that treat bacterial infections. They do not work against viral infections (colds, flu, most sore throats). Classes include penicillins (amoxicillin), cephalosporins, macrolides (azithromycin), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin), and tetracyclines. Antibiotic selection depends on the suspected organism, infection site, local resistance patterns, and patient factors. Completing the full course is important to prevent resistance. Antibiotic resistance is a growing global health threat; antibiotics should only be used when prescribed by a healthcare provider. Common side effects include gastrointestinal upset, allergic reactions, and disruption of normal gut flora.

NONSTEROIDAL ANTI-INFLAMMATORY DRUGS (NSAIDs)
NSAIDs include ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, and aspirin. They work by inhibiting COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes, reducing prostaglandin synthesis. They have analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory properties. Common uses include pain management, fever reduction, and arthritis. Side effects include gastrointestinal irritation and ulceration (risk reduced by taking with food or using gastroprotective agents), renal impairment, cardiovascular risk with long-term use, and platelet inhibition (particularly aspirin). Contraindications include active peptic ulcer, severe renal or hepatic impairment, and third trimester of pregnancy. Individual dosing requirements must be confirmed with a pharmacist or doctor.

SSRI ANTIDEPRESSANTS
Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are first-line pharmacological treatment for depression and anxiety disorders. Common agents include fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine, and citalopram. They work by blocking serotonin reuptake, increasing synaptic serotonin levels. Onset of therapeutic effect typically takes 2-4 weeks. Side effects include nausea, headache, insomnia or somnolence, sexual dysfunction, and activation (anxiety, agitation in early treatment). Rare but serious: serotonin syndrome (with concurrent serotonergic drugs), increased suicidal ideation in young patients (black box warning). Should not be abruptly discontinued due to discontinuation syndrome. Dosage and selection require physician guidance.

ANTIHISTAMINES
Antihistamines block histamine H1 receptors and are used to treat allergic reactions, allergic rhinitis, urticaria, and motion sickness. First-generation antihistamines (diphenhydramine, chlorphenamine) cross the blood-brain barrier, causing sedation — useful for sleep but impairing cognition and coordination. Second-generation antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine) are non-sedating or minimally sedating and are preferred for daytime use. They are generally well-tolerated; common side effects of first-generation include dry mouth, urinary retention, and blurred vision. Antihistamines for severe allergic reactions are adjunctive; epinephrine is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis.
